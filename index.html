Start Here

Two layers, that's the whole trick

A watch is a little machine wearing clothes. See those two layers separately and the hobby opens up.

The machine

Inside is an engine called the movement. It stores energy in a spring and releases it in even ticks to drive the hands. Each movement design has a model name - its caliber. "Caliber 551" tells a collector the size, type, era, and quality in three digits.

The clothes

The case, the dial [the face], hands, crystal, markers - everything you see. This is where most of the value and nearly all the traps live. The big question is always whether the clothes are original.

Quartz

WhatBattery + vibrating crystal. Accurate, cheap, low-romance. Not the target here.
The tellSeconds hand ticks in steps. A mechanical hand sweeps smoothly - that sweep is the giveaway you're looking at a real movement.
Your service paper, decoded: Amplitude = how far the balance swings [250-300° strong]. Beat error = how evenly the tick-tock is spaced [under 0.8ms good]. Rate = seconds gained/lost per day [within ~30s/day is healthy for vintage]. Power reserve = hours on a full wind. The four vital signs.

Patina vs rot: small flat specks on a 70-year-old dial are honest age spotting - cosmetic, stable, and a sign the dial is genuinely original. Active rot spreads, looks raised or bubbling, and eats the dial. Flat = fine. Raised and growing = walk. Tropical dials aged to warm brown are prized when natural, and the single most faked trait in vintage - demand macro shots.
Not Getting Fooled

How to buy vintage

The watch is half of it. The other half is everything the seller left out of frame.

  • Original dial - never repainted. A refinished / redial dial looks too clean for its age and quietly kills value. An honest dial with light spotting beats a flawless repaint.
  • Unpolished case - never buffed down. Sharp, full edges, as the factory made it. Polishing removes metal forever.

Chase the flawless dial and you usually end up with a fake one. Honest imperfection is the price of the real thing.

A franken is built from parts of different watches - one's case, another's dial, a wrong-era movement. Each part may be real; the whole is not honest, and not worth honest money. Confirm dial, case, movement, and hands all belong to the same reference and era. This is what a trusted dealer's reputation buys you.

  • Reference - the model code [your Seamaster: ref 2577]. What the watch is meant to be.
  • Serial - unique, and on most brands it dates the watch [your Constellation's places it ~1960-61].

Cross-check both against dial, case, and movement. One consistent story.

A mechanical watch needs a full service every 3-5 years - a few hundred dollars, more for tricky movements like bumpers. So the real price is the ask plus any service due. A freshly serviced watch earns a premium because that cost and risk is handled.

Trust the source, verify the watch. A dealer you've bought from before lowers risk a lot - it never removes the homework. Even an honest dealer prices a pretty dial high. Still ask for macro shots, the service date, and the caliber. Every time.

The Lay of the Land

Houses worth knowing

Sorted roughly by where they sit. Tap to open. Prices are rough feels for honest entry examples and move around a lot.

A great name and the best value among the heavyweights - similar quality to Rolex, far cheaper.

  • Constellation - chronometer dress line, pie-pan dials. [Your 14381.]
  • Seamaster - everyday and tool, dressy to diver. [Your 2577.]
  • Speedmaster - the chronograph that went to the Moon. The icon. Cal 321 and 861.
  • Key calibers - 30mm family [manual], bumper autos [35x], full-rotor [55x/56x], Speedmaster 321/861.

Most liquid, most recognized, most expensive of the mainstream. Built the waterproof Oyster case.

  • Datejust / Day-Date - the dress staples.
  • Submariner, Explorer, GMT, Daytona - tool legends, now blue-chip money.
  • You pay for engineering, liquidity, and brand gravity. The tax is price - it starts high and climbs.

A storied house punching above its prices - why collectors love it.

  • Polerouter - designed 1954 by a 23-year-old Gérald Genta for SAS polar flights. The microrotor versions [cal 215/218] are the ones to want. [Your current hunt.]
  • Compax / Tri-Compax - superb, still-undervalued chronographs.

A golden-age great whose vintage is some of the best value anywhere. Lovely dress watches and legendary flyback chronographs [13ZN, 30CH] the market still under-prices.

Pre-TAG Heuer built the great racing chronographs.

  • Carrera, Autavia, Monaco [the square McQueen one]. Classic Valjoux movements, very wearable.

Supplied movements to Patek, Vacheron, and AP. Master of the thin and the clever.

  • Reverso [1931] - the Art Deco case that flips to protect the crystal.
  • Memovox - a mechanical alarm watch.
  • Patek Philippe - the most coveted. The Calatrava defines the dress watch; complications set auction records.
  • Vacheron Constantin - oldest manufacture in continuous operation [1755]. Ultra-thin, understated.
  • Audemars Piguet - high complications, and the steel Royal Oak [Genta] that rewrote the rules.
  • IWC - engineering-first; the Mark 11 pilot, antimagnetic Ingenieur.
  • Zenith - the El Primero [1969], one of the first automatic chronographs, high-beat at 36,000/hour.
  • Tudor - Rolex's sibling, once "the affordable Rolex," now hot [snowflake-hand Submariners].
  • Seiko - the serious, affordable entry. The 6105/6309 divers and 6139 chronograph are real icons for a fraction of Swiss money.
